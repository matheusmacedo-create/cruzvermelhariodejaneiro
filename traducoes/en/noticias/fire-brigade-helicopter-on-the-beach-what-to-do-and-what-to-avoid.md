A Fire Brigade helicopter approaches the sand. The scene draws attention — and that is exactly when the risk grows. In the new episode of **PodBradar**, the official podcast of the Rio de Janeiro State Military Fire Brigade (CBMERJ), firefighters from the **Air Operations Group (GOA)** have a warning for anyone who witnesses an operation, especially on the beach: the people around need to move away, not closer. The guidance applies to beachgoers, street vendors, kiosk owners and anyone who happens to be there when the aircraft lands.

## Who this affects

Anyone near an air rescue operation. On Rio de Janeiro's beaches, that means beachgoers, families with children, pet owners, kiosk and beach-tent operators and people walking along the promenade. It also applies on public roads, in wooded areas and at accident sites where the aircraft needs to land or hover low.

A helicopter kicks up a dense cloud of sand, dust and loose objects. Beach umbrellas, tents, towels and bags can be thrown through the air. And there is a specific risk many people do not know about: the **tail rotor**. Because it is smaller and spins at very high speed, it is hard to see and extremely dangerous for anyone who comes close.

## What to do when the aircraft approaches

1. **Move away immediately** from the area where the aircraft is going to land, as soon as you see it coming.
2. **Never walk backwards** towards the helicopter — always look where you are going.
3. **Close tents and umbrellas and gather up light objects** that could be blown away by the rotor wash.
4. **Keep children and pets away** from the operating area, always under direct supervision.
5. **Do not try to get closer to take photos or film.** The selfie can wait; safety cannot.
6. **Never approach the helicopter while the rotors are turning**, even if the aircraft appears to be stationary.
7. **Follow the instructions of the firefighters and lifeguards** on the scene, without arguing or insisting.
8. **Clear the way for the aircraft and the rescue teams to leave** as soon as the operation is over.

The faster the team completes the rescue and releases the helicopter, the faster it is available again for the next emergency. Crowds, onlookers and phones slow that cycle down — and the next call may be on another beach, with another life at stake.

## General good practice at an emergency scene

Besides the guidance specific to air operations, the basic recommendations for any emergency apply: do not crowd around the casualty, do not move injured people without guidance from the team, do not film the rescue and keep access routes clear for emergency vehicles and ambulances. If you were the first to notice the emergency, tell the team clearly what happened and then step back. The best support a bystander can give is space.

## Where to learn more and get prepared

Knowing how to act in the first minutes of an emergency saves lives.
