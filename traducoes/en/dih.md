## The short answer

**International humanitarian law (IHL)** is the body of rules that applies during armed conflict. It does not ask whether a war is just or legal. What it does is set limits: even in combat, not everything is allowed. It is also known as the **law of war** or the **law of armed conflict**.

The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) puts it this way:

> "International humanitarian law is a set of rules which seek, for humanitarian reasons, to limit the effects of armed conflict."

- **Core treaties:** the four Geneva Conventions of 12 August 1949
- **Updated by:** Additional Protocols I and II (1977) and Additional Protocol III (2005)
- **Protects:** people who do not fight, such as civilians and medical staff, and people who no longer fight, such as wounded soldiers and prisoners
- **Limits:** the weapons and methods of warfare that may be used
- **Binds:** every party to a conflict, whoever started it

## What IHL protects

IHL has two main goals.

1. **To protect people.** Those who do not take part in the fighting, such as civilians, health workers and humanitarian workers, and those who no longer take part, such as soldiers who are wounded, sick, shipwrecked or captured, must be respected and treated humanely.
2. **To limit the means and methods of warfare.** Some weapons and tactics are banned, and attacks may never be aimed at civilians.

## The four Geneva Conventions of 1949

The heart of IHL is the four Geneva Conventions, signed on 12 August 1949. They updated older treaties, such as the Geneva Convention of 1929, and each one protects a group of people:

1. **First Convention:** wounded and sick members of the armed forces in the field, on land.
2. **Second Convention:** wounded, sick and shipwrecked members of the armed forces at sea.
3. **Third Convention:** prisoners of war.
4. **Fourth Convention:** civilians in time of war.

All four share one identical article, **Common Article 3**, which sets minimum rules for armed conflicts inside a single country:

> "Persons taking no active part in the hostilities, including members of armed forces who have laid down their arms and those placed hors de combat by sickness, wounds, detention, or any other cause, shall in all circumstances be treated humanely (…)."

## The Additional Protocols of 1977 and 2005

Three Additional Protocols followed:

- **Protocol I (1977):** strengthens the protection of victims of international armed conflicts, between states.
- **Protocol II (1977):** protects victims of non-international armed conflicts, inside a single country.
- **Protocol III (2005):** creates an additional distinctive emblem, the red crystal, alongside the red cross and the red crescent.

Protocol I states one of the central ideas of IHL in a single line:

> "In any armed conflict, the right of the Parties to the conflict to choose methods or means of warfare is not unlimited."

## Distinction, proportionality and precaution

According to the ICRC, the conduct of hostilities rests on three principles:

- **Distinction:** always tell civilians apart from combatants, and attack only combatants and military objectives.
- **Proportionality:** the harm expected to civilians must not be excessive compared with the military advantage anticipated.
- **Precaution:** take constant care to spare the civilian population.

Weapons and methods that cause unnecessary suffering are prohibited.

## IHL and human rights

IHL is not the same as human rights law, but the two work together. Human rights law applies in peace and in war. IHL applies only in armed conflict, and it binds every side equally, no matter who started the fighting. Both protect human life and dignity.

## The emblems: red cross, red crescent, red crystal

The emblems are part of IHL too. In armed conflict, the red cross, the red crescent and the red crystal identify protected medical staff, units and transports. In war or in peace, they also show that a person or an object is linked to the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement. That is why their use is restricted by law.

## A rule nobody knows protects nobody

Spreading IHL in peacetime is a duty written into the treaties themselves. In the First Geneva Convention, states promise to teach it widely:

> "The High Contracting Parties undertake, in time of peace as in time of war, to disseminate the text of the present Convention as widely as possible in their respective countries (…)."

The Red Cross and Red Crescent help with that task in every country. This page is part of it: explaining IHL in plain words to people who have never studied law, and pointing to the official texts.

## IHL in Brazil

Brazil ratified the four Geneva Conventions in 1957, acceded to Protocols I and II in 1992 and ratified Protocol III in 2009. In 2023 the federal government created a National Commission for the Dissemination and Implementation of IHL (Decree No. 11,752), chaired by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The ICRC is invited to its meetings, without a vote.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is international humanitarian law in simple terms?

It is the set of rules that limits the effects of war. It protects people who do not fight or no longer fight, such as civilians, the wounded and prisoners, and it restricts the weapons and methods that may be used.

### What is the difference between IHL and human rights law?

Human rights law applies at all times, in peace and in war. IHL applies only during armed conflict and binds every party to the conflict equally. The two complement each other in protecting human life and dignity.

### What are the four Geneva Conventions about?

The First protects wounded and sick soldiers on land, the Second wounded, sick and shipwrecked members of the armed forces at sea, the Third prisoners of war and the Fourth civilians in time of war.

### What are the three main principles of IHL?

Distinction between civilians and combatants, proportionality between expected civilian harm and military advantage, and precaution to spare the civilian population.

### Who is responsible for spreading IHL?

States promised in the Geneva Conventions to disseminate the treaties as widely as possible, in peace and in war. The Red Cross and Red Crescent help in every country, including the Brazilian Red Cross and its Rio de Janeiro branch.
